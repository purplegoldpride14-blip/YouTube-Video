If you asked Burt Reynolds, near the end of his life, to name his biggest regret, it was not a movie he turned down or a stunt that went wrong. It was one woman, and one relationship he ended more than three decades earlier.

In twenty fifteen, at the peak of writing his own life story, Reynolds sat down to publish a memoir called But Enough About Me. He could have filled it with car chases, cowboy hats, and the biggest box office years of his career. Instead, he gave an entire chapter to a woman he had not spoken to in years. He called her the love of his life. He called her the one who got away, and he kept saying it, in interview after interview, until the phrase followed him into his own obituaries.

Here is the question worth asking. Not whether Burt Reynolds loved Sally Field. He said that part out loud, in print, on camera, for decades, and nobody who watched him say it ever doubted he meant it. The real question is why the woman he was describing kept saying, in her own words, that the man in his memory was not quite the man she remembered living with.

The story starts on a film set in nineteen seventy six. Reynolds was already one of the biggest box office draws in the country. Field was known mostly for television, for the girl next door parts that had followed her since she was a teenager, and she was hungry to be taken seriously as an actress. They were cast opposite each other in a car chase comedy nobody expected to become a phenomenon, a movie called Smokey and the Bandit. They had known each other only a handful of days before the chemistry became obvious to everyone standing near the camera. Reynolds later said it felt less like four days and more like four years. Field, in her own telling, felt the same electricity, the same sense that something had clicked into place faster than either of them could explain.

The movie became one of the highest grossing films of the year, beaten at the box office only by a new space adventure called Star Wars. Off camera, the two leads became one of Hollywood's most watched couples, photographed constantly, asked about constantly, and for a while, the public romance looked exactly like the on screen one. Magazine covers wanted them together. Talk shows wanted them together. Reynolds was, at that point, arguably the single biggest movie star working anywhere in the world, and the fact that he had chosen his co star, in real life, over any of the other women he could have been photographed with, only made the story more irresistible to the public that was watching from the outside.

Over the next five years, they made three more films together, Hooper, The End, and Smokey and the Bandit Two. On screen, the pairing worked every single time. Audiences kept buying the romance because, for a while, it was real. Reynolds proposed marriage more than once during those years. Field turned him down every time, and later said she always sensed his heart was not fully behind the offer, that some part of him wanted the idea of a wife more than the reality of one. That detail matters, because it is the first sign that the two of them were not living inside the same relationship, even while they were living inside the same house, sharing the same films, and being photographed by the same cameras.

For a few years, the cracks stayed almost entirely private. Then came the movie that ended things for good.

In nineteen seventy nine, Field was offered the lead role in a small drama called Norma Rae, about a textile worker who organizes her factory into a union. It was nothing like the glossy comedies that had made her famous, and nothing like anything Reynolds wanted for her. According to Field's own account, he objected to the character, made a demeaning comment about the woman's past, and threw the script back at her rather than simply saying no. When she pushed back and said she wanted the part anyway, he told her she was letting the whole thing get the better of her, as though wanting to stretch as an actress was some kind of overreaction.

Field took the role anyway. She met with the director, signed on, and spent weeks on a factory floor playing a woman with nothing in common with the parts that had made her rich and famous. Reynolds, by her account, then tried to keep her from attending the film's premiere at the Cannes Film Festival, the kind of moment that could define a career. She went regardless. Norma Rae won her the first of two Academy Awards for Best Actress, and it happened despite the man she was living with, not because of any support from him.

That was the real turning point, more than the awards ceremony itself. For years, Field had been introduced as the woman in Burt Reynolds' movies, and by extension, the woman in Burt Reynolds' life. Norma Rae was the first time she stepped fully into her own identity as an actress, on her own terms, against his objections. Field later said that experience marked the beginning of her pulling away from the relationship for good. They stayed together off and on for a little while longer, drifting rather than deciding, but the ending was already written the moment she walked onto that factory set without his blessing. In nineteen eighty two, they split for the final time.
