In January of nineteen ninety nine, security officers at the most secretive intelligence agency in the United States pulled a small, fuzzy, wide eyed toy off an employee's desk and confiscated it like it was a listening device. Because that is exactly what they thought it was.

The agency was the National Security Agency. The toy was a Furby. And for a few strange weeks, one of the most powerful surveillance organizations on earth was more afraid of a thirty five dollar Christmas present than almost anything else on its own property.

Here is the question worth asking. Not whether a talking toy could actually spy on the National Security Agency. It could not, and within days, even the agency's own officials seemed to sense that. The real question is how a plastic and fake fur owl convinced grown adults trained to detect real threats that it might be listening. That question has very little to do with spy craft, and everything to do with how well this toy was built to fake being alive.

The story starts two years earlier, with two inventors named Dave Hampton and Caleb Chung. Hampton had spent years building animatronics and novelty electronics, the kind of small mechanical creatures that move and react just convincingly enough to surprise someone holding them. Chung brought a background in puppetry and film special effects, the discipline of making something lifeless read as alive through nothing but timing and motion. Together, the two of them spent nine months designing the toy on paper and another nine months actually building it, chasing one very specific goal. They did not just want a toy that talked. They wanted a toy that felt like it was discovering the world in real time, right in front of the person holding it, blinking and reacting a half second after you did something, the same way a real animal would.

They partnered with a toy inventor named Richard Levy, who brought the concept to a company called Tiger Electronics. Tiger's leader, Roger Shiffman, bought the rights and unveiled the toy at the American International Toy Fair in New York in early nineteen ninety eight. The response from buyers in the room was immediate. This was not a normal-looking gadget on a shelf full of normal-looking gadgets. It had huge blinking eyes, oversized ears that swiveled and folded, and a beak that opened and closed every time it spoke its own invented language. Buyers who had seen thousands of toy pitches over their careers reportedly stopped to watch this one longer than they meant to.

The toy launched to the public on October second, nineteen ninety eight, priced at around thirty five dollars. And then something happened that Tiger Electronics had not fully prepared for, despite the strong reaction at the Toy Fair months earlier. Demand did not just meet expectations. It broke them completely, the same way a handful of other toy crazes had blindsided manufacturers earlier that same decade.

In its first partial year on shelves, the company sold one point eight million units. By the following year, that number jumped to fourteen million. Over its first three years of production, more than forty million of these toys sold worldwide. During the nineteen ninety eight holiday season specifically, parents could not find them anywhere near the original price. Stores limited customers to one per visit. Local news programs ran segments on which stores had a truck arriving that morning. Resale prices climbed past one hundred dollars, and in the most desperate cases, buyers reported paying several hundred dollars to secondhand sellers just to have one sitting under the tree on Christmas morning.

Part of what made the toy so addictive, for kids and adults alike, was its core trick. Out of the box, it spoke almost entirely in its own made up language, a string of nonsense syllables somewhere between baby talk and a foreign tongue. Owners actually began compiling informal dictionaries of these words, trading translations with each other the way people once traded notes on a foreign language learned on a family trip. But over time, as a child interacted with it, English words gradually started replacing the gibberish, one phrase at a time. It genuinely seemed to be learning how to talk, the same way a small child does, listening, absorbing, and slowly getting better at speaking back. Owners swore their own toy had a personality, a mood, even a sense of humor, despite every unit on the assembly line running the identical program underneath the fur. That illusion was the entire product. It was clever, it was effective, and it was about to cause a problem nobody at the toy company saw coming.

By January of nineteen ninety nine, just over three months after launch, that same illusion reached the National Security Agency, headquartered in Fort Meade, Maryland. On January thirteenth, the agency issued an order banning the toys from its buildings entirely. The Navy's shipyard in Portsmouth, Virginia followed with a similar restriction of its own. Separately, the Federal Aviation Administration weighed in as well, treating the toy the same way it treated any other battery powered electronic device, something that had to be switched off during takeoff and landing rather than left running in a passenger's bag. Three different arms of the federal government, in other words, all decided independently that this specific toy deserved a written policy.
