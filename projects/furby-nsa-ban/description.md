In January of 1999, security officers at the National Security Agency confiscated a small, fuzzy, wide-eyed toy off an employee's desk and treated it like a listening device. The agency was the NSA. The toy was a Furby. For a few strange weeks, one of the most secretive intelligence organizations on Earth was more worried about a thirty-five dollar Christmas present than almost anything else on its own property.

This video tells the full story of how that happened, and why it says more about how convincingly a toy can fake being alive than it does about actual espionage.

**The invention**

The story starts two years before the ban, with two inventors named Dave Hampton and Caleb Chung. Hampton had spent years building animatronics and novelty electronics. Chung brought a background in puppetry and film special effects, the discipline of making something lifeless read as alive through timing and motion alone. Together they spent nine months designing the toy on paper and another nine months building it, chasing one goal: a toy that felt like it was discovering the world in real time, blinking and reacting the way a real animal would.

They partnered with toy inventor Richard Levy, who brought the concept to Tiger Electronics. Tiger's leader, Roger Shiffman, bought the rights and unveiled the toy at the American International Toy Fair in New York in early 1998, where buyers reportedly stopped to watch it longer than they meant to.

**The mania**

The toy launched to the public on October 2, 1998, at around thirty-five dollars, and demand broke every expectation Tiger Electronics had. In its first partial year, the company sold 1.8 million units. The following year that jumped to fourteen million. Over its first three years, more than forty million sold worldwide. During the 1998 holiday season, stores limited customers to one per visit, local news covered incoming shipment trucks, and resale prices climbed past a hundred dollars.

Part of the appeal was the toy's core trick: it launched speaking almost entirely in its own made-up language, then gradually swapped in English words the longer a child interacted with it, creating a convincing illusion of learning.

**The ban**

By January 1999, that same illusion reached the National Security Agency, headquartered in Fort Meade, Maryland. On January 13th, the agency banned the toys from its buildings entirely. The Navy's shipyard in Portsmouth, Virginia followed with a similar restriction, and the Federal Aviation Administration treated it like any other battery-powered device that had to be switched off during takeoff and landing.

At the NSA, the reasoning was literal: recording devices of any kind are forbidden inside its facilities, and because the toy appeared to learn new words from its surroundings, officials worried it might be quietly recording classified conversations and repeating them back later.

**The truth**

Tiger Electronics pushed back immediately. Roger Shiffman said the toy had no ability to record anything. Dave Hampton demonstrated publicly that its microphone could only detect simple sound patterns like claps, nothing close to capturing human speech. Inside every unit sat a basic processor and speech synthesis chip, no more advanced than other talking toys of that era. Its vocabulary wasn't learned from its environment at all; it was hardcoded to swap out its made-up language for English on a fixed internal schedule regardless of what anyone said to it. Faced with that explanation, the NSA quietly walked the ban back.

**What happened next**

The original mania cooled by around 2002, after more than forty million units sold. But it wasn't the end of the story. In 2005, Hasbro, which had acquired Tiger Electronics, relaunched the toy with new features. Further versions followed in 2012 with animated screen eyes, in 2016 with smartphone connectivity, and in 2023 with voice activation and more than six hundred built-in responses. By 2025, it was a finalist for the National Toy Hall of Fame, more than a quarter century after it briefly frightened one of the country's most secretive agencies.

What actually happened in that strange month wasn't a security breach. It was two inventors building something so convincing that it fooled the exact people whose job was not to be fooled, decades before anyone worried about a smart speaker listening in the next room.

By watching this video, you'll learn who actually invented the toy at the center of this story, why three separate parts of the federal government independently restricted it, what was really inside its plastic shell, and how the same illusion that caused a security scare in 1999 turned into one of the most enduring toy franchises in American history.
