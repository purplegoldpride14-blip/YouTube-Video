Burt Reynolds spent five straight years, 1978 to 1982, as the number one box office star on the planet. He also spent the decades after that giving almost all of it back, one confident decision at a time. This is the story of how the man behind Deliverance and Smokey and the Bandit ended up selling his own trophies to strangers in a Las Vegas ballroom, and why the loss he talked about most in his final years was never about money at all.

The Rise

It starts in 1957, with a Florida State football scholarship and a car accident that took his spleen and ended his athletic career before it properly began. A drama teacher at a junior college in Palm Beach cast him in a play almost by accident, and that same stubborn refusal to accept a closed door carried Burt Reynolds from a college stage into Deliverance in 1972, then into Smokey and the Bandit in 1977 alongside Sally Field, a film shot on a modest budget that pulled in over a hundred million dollars. A friendship with former stuntman Hal Needham is part of how that film happened at all. By the height of his fame, Reynolds was earning four to five million dollars a picture and had been voted the industry's top box office draw for five consecutive years.

The Collapse

The video walks through exactly how that fortune came apart. A chain of restaurants swallowed close to thirty million dollars. In 1990, Reynolds borrowed four million dollars from the television network behind his show Evening Shade, betting on syndication money that never arrived after the show was cancelled one season too early. By December 1996, he filed for Chapter 11 bankruptcy with roughly six point six million dollars in assets against eleven point two million dollars in debt, owing creditors that included the Internal Revenue Service, Chase Bank, his own talent agencies, and even the man who made his toupees. His 1993 divorce from Loni Anderson added a brutal financial and personal toll on top of it, eventually pushing him to auction off his own Hollywood memorabilia in 2014, including a horse-and-rider statue and the wedding stagecoach from his marriage to Anderson, just to stay solvent.

The Regret, and the Redemption

Money is not what the video argues actually broke him. In his own memoir, Reynolds wrote that Sally Field, his Smokey and the Bandit co-star, was the one relationship he regretted losing, calling himself "an arrogant jerk" for letting it go. That same stubbornness nearly cost him his last great role: he turned down Boogie Nights seven times before director Paul Thomas Anderson talked him into it, a performance that earned Reynolds his only Academy Award nomination at sixty-one years old.

The Ending

The years after Boogie Nights were not a comeback. A quintuple heart bypass in 2010, smaller roles, and a slow physical decline led to his death from a heart attack on September 6, 2018, in a Florida hospital, at age eighty-two. Those close to him said his health had clearly been failing, and that his death was still somehow unexpected.
