On September 18, 2009, a television show ended after 72 years on the air — and almost nobody noticed. This video is the story of the single longest-running scripted program in the history of broadcasting, and why its ending came and went without any of the fanfare television usually saves for something that old.

**The Origin**

The story starts in 1937 with Irna Phillips. Two years earlier, at nineteen years old, Phillips had given birth to a stillborn child. In the aftermath, she found comfort in the sermons of a preacher whose church centered on the brotherhood of man, and that feeling became the seed of a new kind of radio program. She built a fifteen-minute serial around a widowed minister, Reverend John Ruthledge, a man his neighbors turned to whenever their lives went sideways. In his study sat a lamp, always lit, meant to represent the small guidance he offered anyone who walked through his door. The show was named after that lamp. It debuted on NBC radio on January 25, 1937.

Phillips is barely remembered outside television history books today, despite essentially inventing the format the entire soap opera genre still runs on. She later created another long-running serial that stayed on the air one full year longer, closing out in 2010 — but even that show fell more than a decade short of this one's total run, because nothing else Phillips built carried this show's fifteen-year radio head start. Cliffhangers timed to the end of every episode, storylines that unfold over months instead of resolving cleanly — those techniques trace directly back to decisions she made building this one show.

**Seventy-Two Years, Nearly Nineteen Thousand Episodes**

The show moved to CBS radio in 1947, then jumped to CBS television on June 30, 1952, running in both formats side by side before radio faded out. From its first radio broadcast to its final television episode, it stayed on the air for 72 consecutive years — 15,762 episodes on television alone, and more than 18,500 counting the radio years. Guinness World Records recognized it as the longest-running scripted drama in broadcasting history, a record no other program has come close to since.

**The Actors Who Passed Through**

A production running at that pace accumulates people, and this is where the story gets genuinely strange. Long before he was a movie star, Kevin Bacon spent 1980 and 1981 playing a teenage alcoholic named Tim Werner on this show, years before Diner and Footloose. In 1989, a young Calista Flockhart took a minor role as a babysitter, eight years before Ally McBeal made her a household name. James Earl Jones, Cicely Tyson, Allison Janney, and a teenage Hayden Panettiere all passed through its cast before any of them were famous — because daytime drama was one of the only places an unproven actor could work steadily in front of a live audience of millions while learning the job.

Not everyone was passing through. Kim Zimmer played the same character, Reva Shayne, across nearly two thousand episodes spanning more than two decades, leaving and returning to the role twice before playing her one final time in the show's very last episode.

**How It Ended**

In April 2009, CBS cancelled the show. By then it had become the lowest-rated of the eight daytime dramas still airing on American network television, averaging 2.17 million viewers and a 0.9 rating among women 18-49 — both sliding hard, down 18% and 25% respectively from the season before. Production wrapped its final scenes on August 11, 2009, and the finale aired five weeks later with a one-year flash-forward. No primetime special. No retrospective on a competing network.

Within three years, three more of the eight daytime dramas still airing at the time were also cancelled. By the middle of the 2010s, only three network daytime soaps remained, down from more than a dozen at the genre's peak. This show wasn't an isolated case — it was the biggest domino in a collapse already underway.
